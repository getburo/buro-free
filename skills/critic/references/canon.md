# Critic · canon — the sources behind the lenses

Compressed reference for the `buro:critic` seat. Tools, not rules.

## Judge on the work's own terms

The oldest rule of serious criticism (from Goethe's three questions onward): first ask **what
the work is trying to do**, then **how well it does it**, then **was it worth doing**. Judging a
work against an ambition it never had — damning a farce for not being profound — is a category
error, not a critique. Meet the work where it stands, *then* measure.

## The standard / comparison class

A verdict means nothing without a **comparison class**: the best of the work's own form. "Good"
is temperature; "a solid but minor entry in the locked-room tradition, well short of [the
form's best]" is a judgement. Name the shelf, then place the work on it. The critic's authority
comes from knowing the form deeply enough to locate the work within it.

## Intention vs achievement

Separate what the work **reached for** from what it **earned**:
- Ambitious reach + full achievement = major.
- Ambitious reach + partial achievement = honourable failure (often more interesting than a
  safe success).
- Modest reach + full achievement = minor success.
- Modest reach + partial achievement = forgettable.

Naming which quadrant, honestly, is most of the verdict.

## Originality vs competence

Does the work do something its form **hasn't**, or assemble the familiar **competently**?
Neither is automatically praise or damnation — a fresh failure and a competent pastiche both
exist. The critic's job is to *not be fooled*: to catch the derivative wearing originality's
clothes, and to notice genuine novelty that looks modest.

## The honesty / posturing test

Great criticism has a nose for the **fake**: edginess as a pose, sentimentality reaching for a
cheap tear, the profound-*seeming* with nothing beneath, the technically impressive that means
nothing. The test is whether the work **means** what it performs, or performs an effect it
hasn't earned. This is the same honesty law the making seats obey, applied to the finished whole.

## Evidence-based verdict

Every claim is anchored to a **specific moment** — a scene, a line, a mechanic, a frame, a
cut. "The film is hollow" is a vibe; "the film is hollow — the climax (the rooftop scene)
resolves the plot but not the theme the first act promised" is criticism. Quote the work, or
it isn't a verdict.

## The Braintrust discipline — deliver the verdict so it's usable

From Ed Catmull's *Creativity, Inc.* (2014): Pixar's Braintrust gives notes that **diagnose**
rather than **prescribe**. Carried into a single critical verdict:

- **Name the problem, not the cure.** A good note says *what* didn't work and *why*, with
  evidence, and stops. Prescribing the fix steals the author's ownership of their own work — and
  is usually wrong anyway, because the critic sees the symptom clearly and the remedy poorly. The
  verdict locates the ceiling; the making seat finds the door.
- **The person is not the work.** Candor is aimed at the *piece*, never the maker. "You're a hack"
  is contempt; "this beat doesn't earn its turn" is criticism.
- **Plus, don't only veto.** Name what is *alive* in the work as precisely as what's dead —
  building on the real thing is both more useful and more honest than a pile of condemnations.

**Seam.** `buro:creative-director` owns the Braintrust as a *room and a process*. Here it's the
delivery-discipline of one verdict: judge hard, prove it from the work, and hand the fix back to
the author who owns it.

## The critic's own vices (guard against them)

- **Cruelty for sport:** performing contempt to sound discerning. Harshness must be *earned and
  useful*, not a display.
- **Flattery / conflict-avoidance:** praise that names nothing, to be liked or safe.
- **The category error:** judging against the wrong form.
- **Reviewing the pitch:** grading the intention or the ambition instead of the artifact.

The honest verdict — high or low — is specific, defended, and about the *work*, not the critic.
