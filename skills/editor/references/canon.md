# Editing · canon — the sources behind the lenses

Compressed craft reference for the `buro:editor` seat. Tools, not rules; the SKILL.md
lenses are how they get applied.

## The three edits — distinct passes, in order

1. **Developmental / structural edit** — the big shape. Does the piece deliver the promise
   it made? Is the order right? What whole sections earn their place, and what should be
   cut, merged, or moved? Never line-polish a paragraph you might delete — structure first.
2. **Line edit** — the sentence and paragraph. Clarity, rhythm, flabby-word removal, dead
   transitions, mixed metaphors, the reader's felt pace. The prose level, not the typo level.
3. **Copy-edit / proof** — grammar, consistency, spelling, house style. Last, because it's
   wasted on text that's about to change.

Doing these out of order is the most common way editing wastes effort.

## "Kill your darlings"

Attributed through Faulkner to Arthur Quiller-Couch ("Murder your darlings"). The line you
love most is the *first* to suspect, because you're keeping it for you, not the reader. A
beautiful sentence that doesn't serve the piece is worse than a plain one that does — its
beauty is a distraction with a good disguise.

## Omit needless words (Strunk & White) / info-style (Ilyahov)

Strunk's rule: "Omit needless words. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a
paragraph no unnecessary sentences." The English canon and the Russian **info-style
(информационный стиль)** of Maxim Ilyahov agree on the mechanism: strip stop-words,
hedges, and throat-clearing; every claim must be backed by a fact or cut. Info-style adds a
sharp test for non-fiction: replace evaluative adjectives ("very effective," "innovative")
with the concrete fact that would justify them — or delete the claim.

**Before:** "It is important to note that the results were, in general, quite positive."
**After:** "The results were positive." → or, better: "Conversions rose 12%."

## Provost — sentence-length rhythm

Gary Provost's demonstration: a run of same-length sentences deadens; varied length sings.

> This sentence has five words. Here are five more words. Five-word sentences are fine. But
> several together become monotonous. Listen to what is happening. The writing is getting
> boring. The sound of it drones. It's like a stuck record. The ear demands some variety.
> Now listen. I vary the sentence length, and I create music.

An editor hunts the drone and breaks it.

## The read-aloud test

Read the passage aloud (or sub-vocalise). Where you stumble, the reader stumbles: a
run-on, a missing beat, a tongue-twist, a clause that arrives in the wrong order. The ear
catches what the eye skims.

## How to give an edit note

A note is a **change + a reason**, aimed at a specific place — never a mood.

- ✗ "This drags." → ✓ "Cut ¶3–4; the point lands in ¶2 and the examples repeat it."
- ✗ "Feels off." → ✓ "The claim in line 6 ('the best on the market') has no proof — cite the
  benchmark or drop the superlative."
- ✗ "Make it punchier." → ✓ "Break the 40-word second sentence at 'because'; the setup and
  the payoff are fighting in one breath."

## In service of the author's voice

The edit sharpens *their* voice and *their* argument. The instant the text starts sounding
like the editor, the edit has failed — that's rewriting, not editing. When in doubt,
propose the cut and let the author keep the veto: show the removal (tracked-change style)
rather than silently replacing their words with yours.
