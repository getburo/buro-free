# Directing · canon — the sources behind the lenses

Compressed reference for the `buro:director` seat. Tools, not rules.

## Mise-en-scène

Everything placed within the frame and how it's photographed: blocking, set, light, colour,
and camera. The director's core language — the scene's meaning is *staged*, not just spoken.
Ask of any element in frame: why is it there, and what does it say.

## The 180-degree rule & eyeline match

Draw an imaginary line between the two subjects; keep the camera on one side so screen
directions stay consistent (she looks right, he looks left, and they seem to face each
other). **Cross it** and the geography flips — disorienting unless done on purpose (to mark
a turn, a break, a loss of control). **Eyeline match:** a character's gaze and the reverse
must agree, or the audience subconsciously loses the space.

## Shot sizes & angles as meaning

- **Wide / long:** context, isolation, smallness against the world.
- **Medium:** the social distance of conversation.
- **Close-up:** intimacy, emphasis — the most emphatic tool; spend it on the beat that earns
  it, and it goes dead if overused.
- **Extreme close:** a detail loaded past its size (a trembling hand, a tell).
- **Low angle:** grants power/threat. **High angle:** shrinks, exposes, pities. **Eye level:**
  neutral, equal. **Dutch tilt:** wrongness, instability.

## Camera movement vocabulary — each with a job

- **Push in / dolly:** intensify, close the distance to a realisation.
- **Pull out:** reveal isolation or context; abandon.
- **Crane / boom:** scope, God's-eye, or a reveal of scale.
- **Handheld:** nerves, immediacy, documentary truth.
- **Locked / static:** control, stillness, a trap; power in refusing to move.
- **Whip pan / snap:** energy, connection between two things.

The fault to hunt: movement chosen for "cinematic" feel with no job.

## Tempo-rhythm (Stanislavski)

The scene's *inner pace* — the emotional velocity, distinct from literal speed. A scene must
*change* tempo to build: hold on a face past comfort, then accelerate; let a beat breathe,
then compress. One metronomic pace can't build tension. Directors think in the tempo-rhythm
of the whole scene, not just shot to shot.

## Blocking as subtext

The actors' positions and moves are dialogue without words. Who approaches and who retreats;
who sits while others stand (and owns the room by it); who won't turn to face whom; the
distance that closes or opens across the scene. Watch the scene muted: if the bodies tell
the story, the blocking is working. If actors just stand and deliver, half the tools are
unused.

## Coverage vs. designed shooting

**Coverage** = shooting the scene from many standard setups (master, singles, overs) to
assemble later. Useful as safety, dangerous as a *substitute for a decision*: shoot
everything and you've often decided nothing, and it shows as a scene with no point of view.
A **designed** plan gives every setup a job in the intended cut — and leaves the editor
something shaped to work with.
