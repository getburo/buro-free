# Art Direction · canon — the sources behind the lenses

Compressed reference for the `buro:art-director` seat. Tools, not rules.

## Limited palette & colour meaning

A controlled palette reads as intentional and rich; an unlimited one reads as noise and
cheapness. Techniques: a dominant + secondary + one **accent** (the accent used sparingly to
direct the eye and *mean* something — danger, the hero, the goal). Colour scripts (Pixar) plan
the palette across a whole film so colour carries emotion scene to scene. Ask of every hue:
what is its job? A colour with no job is clutter. (For chart colour specifically — the four jobs
and the validated palette — that's `buro:dataviz`; here it's the world's palette.)

## Shape / silhouette language

A consistent visual grammar: round vs angular, ornate vs austere, organic vs geometric. Shapes
*signal* — soft rounds read safe/friendly, sharp angles read dangerous, verticals read
grand/authoritarian. Characters and props should be distinguishable and meaningful **in
silhouette** alone. Mixed, unrelated shape grammars fracture a world.

## Coherence — the one-world rule

The art director's real product is **sameness of world** across every asset: one level of
stylisation, one rendering logic, one light. The most common failure of amateur work is
several individually-good styles stapled together. Coherence beats the single best image —
a uniformly strong world reads as designed; a brilliant outlier reads as a mistake.

## Reference-board discipline

Specificity comes from references, and from knowing **what to take and what to leave**. A mood
board is not decoration — it's a decision record: this era's silhouettes, that film's palette,
this material. Without it, work drifts to the generic default of its genre ("fantasy game
#400"). With it, and with a clear editorial hand over it, the look gets particular.

## Lighting & mood

Light is half the look: key direction, contrast ratio (hard/soft), temperature (warm/cold),
and atmosphere (fog, haze, bloom) set mood and separate depth planes. Consistent lighting logic
holds a world together; mood-less or inconsistent light flattens even strong design.

## Visual hierarchy — why "make it pop" is a subtraction problem

The eye goes to contrast: the brightest, most saturated, most detailed, most isolated element.
You make a subject "pop" not by adding to it but by **holding everything else back** — desaturate
the surroundings, drop their contrast, simplify around the focal point. "Make it pop" is a
symptom; the cure is almost always subtraction, not addition.

## The honest limit

This seat **defines and briefs** the visual system — palette values, shape rules, described
style frames, do/don't. It does **not** paint the final pixels; the render is dispatched to a
human artist or an image tool, guided by the bible. Saying so plainly is part of the honesty
law — the studio briefs the look, and names where the human hand or the tool takes over.
