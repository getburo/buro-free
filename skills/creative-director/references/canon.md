# Creative Direction · canon — the sources behind the lenses

Compressed reference for the `buro:creative-director` seat. Tools, not rules.

## Vision and the one-sentence test

A work needs a **vision** small enough to say in a sentence and big enough to align every
discipline: what it's *about* and how it should *feel*. ("A tender game about loss where every
system refuses to let you win.") If it can't be said, there's nothing for art, writing, sound,
and design to cohere *to*. The one sentence is the north-star every seat checks its work against.

## The through-line

Borrowed from Stanislavski (the *skvoznoe deistvie*, the through-action) and scaled to a whole
production: a single spine that runs through every part. Each discipline should express the same
vision — the art, the writing, the score, the level design all saying the one thing. A seat whose
excellent output doesn't serve the through-line is *drift*, however good it is alone.

## Tonal coherence

The most common multi-seat failure is **tonal**: each part is competent, but a comic script sits
under grimdark art, or a tense score plays over a cozy level. Tone is felt instantly and
holistically; a split reads as "something's off" long before anyone can name it. The creative
director's ear for tone across disciplines is a primary instrument.

## The showrunner model

Television's answer to multi-discipline coherence: a **showrunner** holds the vision across
writers, directors, designers, and composers who each rotate through — ensuring the season is one
show, not a dozen good episodes pulling apart. The lesson for any studio: one taste must own the
whole, or excellence in the parts fractures the work.

## Resolving cross-discipline conflict

When two disciplines genuinely conflict (the writer's slow scene vs the editor's cut; the
concept's detail vs the readability's restraint), resolve on **what the work needs**, defended
with evidence from the piece — not by hierarchy, seniority, or who argued longest. Name the real
trade-off, hear both seats, and make a call you could justify to the loser.

## The consilium

Before overriding any seat's craft, **hear it** — put each discipline's expert view on the table.
A vision imposed without consulting the seats is a guess wearing authority. (This mirrors
`buro:usability`'s consilium of specialists, at the level of a whole production.) The synthesis is
stronger for having reconciled real expert input, not bypassed it.

## Vision-as-servant vs auteur-ego

The vision exists to serve the **work**, not to monument the director. "Because I say so" is ego,
not direction. The honest test for any call: *could you defend it, with evidence from the piece,
to someone who disagreed?* And the courage the role demands: to **change the vision** when the
work reveals it was wrong, and to **cut a darling** — even a brilliant part — when it breaks the
whole. Coherence is worth more than any single excellent piece.

## The Braintrust (Pixar)

Ed Catmull's account of how Pixar keeps a film honest without ruling by fiat (*Creativity, Inc.*,
2014). A standing group of trusted directors and story people watches a cut and gives *candid*
feedback. Its four disciplines:

- **Candor over politeness.** The room says what isn't working; comfort that spares egos spares
  the flaws too. Candor is possible precisely because the group has **no authority** — it can't
  order a change, so the director can hear the note without having to defend a command.
- **Diagnose, don't prescribe.** A good note identifies the *problem* ("this beat lost me, and
  here's why") and leaves the *solution* to the director, who owns the film. A note that dictates
  the fix is usually wrong and always steals ownership.
- **The work is not the maker.** "Early on, all of our movies suck." Every film starts as an
  **ugly baby** — ungainly, unformed. The job is to *protect* it while it's fragile **and** be
  brutally candid as it matures; attacking the baby, or coddling it past the point it can take the
  truth, both kill it.
- **Plussing.** Borrowed from improv and Disney: build on an idea (*"yes, and…"*) before you veto
  it — add before you subtract, so a note opens a door instead of only closing one.

**The authority sits with the director, not the room.** The Braintrust advises; the director
decides — the same law as vision-as-servant: the review serves the work, and no one rules by rank.

**Seam.** This seat owns the Braintrust as a *room and a process* — where the vision comes from,
and how a whole work is held coherent under candid review. Delivering a *judgement against the best
of the form* — a single defended verdict — is `buro:critic`, which carries the Braintrust's
delivery-discipline (name the problem, don't prescribe the cure) into one review.
